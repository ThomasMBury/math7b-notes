    Section 6.1 Powers of Sine and Cosine

    We now consider integrals of the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int \sin^m(x)\cos^n(x)\,dx. \end{equation*}
    The appropriate strategy depends on whether the powers \(m\) and \(n\) are odd or even.
    🔗

    Subsection 6.1.1 Useful Trigonometric Identities

    The Pythagorean identity is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sin^2x+\cos^2x=1. \end{equation*}
    This allows us to write
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sin^2x=1-\cos^2x \end{equation*}
    or
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \cos^2x=1-\sin^2x. \end{equation*}
    When both powers are even, the power-reduction identities are useful:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \cos^2x = \frac{1}{2}(1+\cos 2x), \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \sin^2x = \frac{1}{2}(1-\cos 2x). \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Subsection 6.1.2 Case 1: The Power of Sine Is Odd

    If the power of sine is odd, save one factor of \(\sin x\text{.}\) Convert the remaining even power of sine to cosine using
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sin^2x=1-\cos^2x, \end{equation*}
    and then use the substitution \(u=\cos x\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 6.1.1. An odd power of sine.

    Evaluate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\sin^7x\cos^2x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Save one factor of \(\sin x\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sin^7x = \sin^6x\sin x = (\sin^2x)^3\sin x. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\sin^7x\cos^2x\,dx = \int (1-\cos^2x)^3 \cos^2x \sin x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u=\cos x, \qquad du=-\sin x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -\int(1-u^2)^3u^2\,du. \end{equation*}
    Expanding,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -\int \left( u^2-3u^4+3u^6-u^8 \right)\,du. \end{equation*}
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -\frac{1}{3}u^3 + \frac{3}{5}u^5 - \frac{3}{7}u^7 + \frac{1}{9}u^9 + C. \end{equation*}
    Substituting back,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\sin^7x\cos^2x\,dx = -\frac{1}{3}\cos^3x + \frac{3}{5}\cos^5x - \frac{3}{7}\cos^7x + \frac{1}{9}\cos^9x + C. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.1.3 Case 2: The Power of Cosine Is Odd

    If the power of cosine is odd, save one factor of \(\cos x\text{.}\) Convert the remaining even power of cosine using
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \cos^2x=1-\sin^2x, \end{equation*}
    and then use \(u=\sin x\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 6.1.2. An odd power of cosine.

    Evaluate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\sin^{100}x\cos^3x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Write
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \cos^3x = \cos^2x\cos x. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\sin^{100}x\cos^3x\,dx = \int \sin^{100}x (1-\sin^2x) \cos x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u=\sin x, \qquad du=\cos x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int u^{100}(1-u^2)\,du = \int \left( u^{100}-u^{102} \right)\,du. \end{equation*}
    Hence,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\sin^{100}x\cos^3x\,dx = \frac{\sin^{101}x}{101} - \frac{\sin^{103}x}{103} + C. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.1.4 Case 3: Both Powers Are Even

    If both powers are even, use the power-reduction identities to reduce the powers.
    🔗

    Example 6.1.3. Even powers of sine and cosine.

    Evaluate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\sin^2x\cos^2x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Use the power-reduction identities:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sin^2x\cos^2x = \frac{1}{4} (1-\cos 2x)(1+\cos 2x). \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sin^2x\cos^2x = \frac{1}{4} \left( 1-\cos^2 2x \right). \end{equation*}
    Apply the power-reduction identity again:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \cos^2 2x = \frac{1}{2}(1+\cos 4x). \end{equation*}
    Hence,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\sin^2x\cos^2x\,dx = \frac{1}{8} \int (1-\cos 4x)\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\sin^2x\cos^2x\,dx = \frac{x}{8} - \frac{1}{32}\sin 4x + C. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 6.1.4. If both powers are odd.

    If both powers are odd, either of the first two strategies can be used. It is often easiest to save a factor from the function having the smaller power.
