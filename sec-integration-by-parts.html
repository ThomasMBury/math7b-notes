    Section 5.3 Integration by Parts

    The substitution rule can be viewed as reversing the chain rule. Integration by parts is based on reversing another differentiation rule: the product rule.
    🔗
    Recall that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}\left(u(x)v(x)\right) = u'(x)v(x)+u(x)v'(x). \end{equation*}
    Integrating both sides with respect to \(x\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} uv = \int u'v\,dx + \int uv'\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Rearranging gives the integration-by-parts formula.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.3.1. Integration by Parts.

    \begin{equation*} \int uv'\,dx = uv-\int vu'\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Equivalently, using differential notation,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int u\,dv = uv-\int v\,du. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The goal is to transform the original integral into a new integral that is easier to evaluate.
    🔗

    Example 5.3.2. A first integration-by-parts example.

    Evaluate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int xe^x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Choose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u=x, \qquad dv=e^x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} du=dx, \qquad v=e^x. \end{equation*}
    Applying integration by parts,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int xe^x\,dx = xe^x-\int e^x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int xe^x\,dx = xe^x-e^x+C. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.3.3. Choosing \(u\) and \(dv\).

    We must choose \(dv\) so that we can find its antiderivative \(v\text{.}\) We would also like the derivative \(du\) to simplify the remaining integral.
    🔗
    🔗
    A poor choice can make the new integral harder than the original one. For example, in \(\int xe^x\,dx\text{,}\) choosing \(u=e^x\) and \(dv=x\,dx\) produces an integral involving \(x^2e^x\text{,}\) which is more complicated than where we started.
    🔗

    Example 5.3.4. Applying integration by parts twice.

    Evaluate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int x^2\sin x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Choose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u=x^2, \qquad dv=\sin x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} du=2x\,dx, \qquad v=-\cos x. \end{equation*}
    Integration by parts gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int x^2\sin x\,dx = -x^2\cos x + 2\int x\cos x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    The remaining integral also requires integration by parts. Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u=x, \qquad dv=\cos x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} du=dx, \qquad v=\sin x. \end{equation*}
    Hence
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int x\cos x\,dx = x\sin x-\int\sin x\,dx = x\sin x+\cos x. \end{equation*}
    Substituting into the original expression,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int x^2\sin x\,dx = -x^2\cos x + 2x\sin x + 2\cos x + C. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.3.5. Check by differentiating.

    Integration-by-parts calculations can involve several steps. Differentiating the final answer is a useful way to check that it reproduces the original integrand.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.3.6. A clever use of integration by parts.

    Evaluate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\ln x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Although the integrand does not visibly contain a product, write
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\ln x\,dx = \int 1\cdot\ln x\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Choose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u=\ln x, \qquad dv=dx. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} du=\frac{1}{x}\,dx, \qquad v=x. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\ln x\,dx = x\ln x - \int x\frac{1}{x}\,dx = x\ln x-x+C. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.1 Integration by Parts for Definite Integrals

    With limits of integration, the integration-by-parts formula becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b uv'\,dx = [uv]_a^b - \int_a^b vu'\,dx. \end{equation*}
    The boundary term \([uv]_a^b\) must be evaluated at both endpoints.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.2 Choosing an Integration Method

    Determining whether to use substitution or integration by parts often requires practice. A useful question is whether differentiating part of the integrand simplifies the problem, or whether an inner function and its derivative are both present.
    🔗
    For example, an integral of the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int xe^{x^2}\,dx \end{equation*}
    suggests substitution because the derivative of \(x^2\) appears in the integrand. In contrast,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int x e^{2x}\,dx \end{equation*}
    is naturally handled by integration by parts.
    🔗

    Remark 5.3.7. A rule of thumb for choosing \(u\).

    A common mnemonic is LIATE. When possible, choose \(u\) from the first applicable category in the following order:
    🔗
    1. Logarithmic functions,
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Inverse trigonometric functions,
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Algebraic functions,
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Trigonometric functions,
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Exponential functions.
      🔗
      🔗
    This is a useful guideline rather than a mathematical rule.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.3.3 Combining Substitution and Integration by Parts

    Some integrals require more than one technique.
    🔗

    Example 5.3.8. Using substitution and integration by parts together.

    Evaluate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int x^3e^{x^2}\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    The expression \(x^2\) inside the exponential suggests beginning with a substitution. Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} w=x^2. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} dw=2x\,dx, \end{equation*}
    so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\,dw. \end{equation*}
    Since \(x^2=w\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int x^3e^{x^2}\,dx = \frac{1}{2} \int we^w\,dw. \end{equation*}
    Now apply integration by parts to \(\int we^w\,dw\text{.}\) Choose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u=w, \qquad dv=e^w\,dw. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} du=dw, \qquad v=e^w. \end{equation*}
    Hence
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int we^w\,dw = we^w-e^w+C. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int x^3e^{x^2}\,dx = \frac{1}{2} \left( x^2e^{x^2}-e^{x^2} \right) +C. \end{equation*}
    Factoring gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int x^3e^{x^2}\,dx = \frac{1}{2} e^{x^2}(x^2-1)+C. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
