    Section 5.4 Rational Functions and Partial Fractions

    Some rational functions are difficult to integrate directly but can be rewritten as sums of simpler rational functions.
    🔗

    Definition 5.4.1. Rational Function.

    A rational function is a quotient of two polynomials:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Definition 5.4.2. Proper Rational Function.

    A rational function
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \end{equation*}
    is proper if the degree of \(P\) is less than the degree of \(Q\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.4.3. First check whether the rational function is proper.

    Partial-fraction decomposition is applied to proper rational functions. If the numerator has degree greater than or equal to the denominator, first use polynomial long division.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.4.4. Long division before integration.

    Rewrite
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{3x^3+8x^2-x+7}{x+3} \end{equation*}
    as a polynomial plus a proper rational function.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Polynomial long division gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{3x^3+8x^2-x+7}{x+3} = 3x^2-x+2+\frac{1}{x+3}. \end{equation*}
    The remaining rational term is now proper.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.4.1 Distinct Linear Factors

    Suppose the denominator factors into distinct linear factors. We assign one constant numerator to each factor.
    🔗

    Example 5.4.5. A basic partial-fraction decomposition.

    Decompose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{x(x-1)}. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Write
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{x(x-1)} = \frac{A}{x} + \frac{B}{x-1}. \end{equation*}
    Multiply through by \(x(x-1)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1=A(x-1)+Bx. \end{equation*}
    Expanding,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1=(A+B)x-A. \end{equation*}
    Matching coefficients gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A+B=0, \qquad -A=1. \end{equation*}
    Thus
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A=-1, \qquad B=1. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{x(x-1)} = -\frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{x-1}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Once the decomposition is known, integration becomes straightforward:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\frac{1}{x(x-1)}\,dx = \ln|x-1|-\ln|x|+C. \end{equation*}

    Example 5.4.6. Two distinct linear factors.

    Decompose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2x+1}{(x-1)(x+3)}. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Write
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2x+1}{(x-1)(x+3)} = \frac{A}{x-1} + \frac{B}{x+3}. \end{equation*}
    Multiplying by \((x-1)(x+3)\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2x+1 = A(x+3)+B(x-1). \end{equation*}
    One method is to compare coefficients. Expanding gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2x+1 = (A+B)x+(3A-B). \end{equation*}
    Hence
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A+B=2, \qquad 3A-B=1. \end{equation*}
    Solving,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A=\frac{3}{4}, \qquad B=\frac{5}{4}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2x+1}{(x-1)(x+3)} = \frac{3}{4(x-1)} + \frac{5}{4(x+3)}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 5.4.7. Strategic values of \(x\).

    Instead of comparing coefficients, it is often faster to substitute values of \(x\) that make individual factors vanish. For the previous example, \(x=1\) isolates \(A\text{,}\) while \(x=-3\) isolates \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.4.2 Repeated Linear Factors

    If a linear factor is repeated, the decomposition must contain a term for every power of that factor.
    🔗
    For example, a denominator containing \((x+b)^n\) requires terms of the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{A_1}{x+b} + \frac{A_2}{(x+b)^2} + \cdots + \frac{A_n}{(x+b)^n}. \end{equation*}

    Example 5.4.8. A repeated linear factor.

    Evaluate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\frac{x}{(x+1)^2}\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Write
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x}{(x+1)^2} = \frac{A}{x+1} + \frac{B}{(x+1)^2}. \end{equation*}
    Multiplying by \((x+1)^2\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=A(x+1)+B. \end{equation*}
    Comparing coefficients gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A=1, \qquad B=-1. \end{equation*}
    Thus
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x}{(x+1)^2} = \frac{1}{x+1} - \frac{1}{(x+1)^2}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\frac{x}{(x+1)^2}\,dx = \ln|x+1| + \frac{1}{x+1} + C. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 5.4.3 Irreducible Quadratic Factors

    A quadratic factor that has no real roots cannot be factored into real linear factors. Such a factor is called irreducible over the real numbers.
    🔗
    For an irreducible quadratic factor, the corresponding numerator in a partial-fraction decomposition is linear. For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{Ax+B}{x^2+1}. \end{equation*}

    Example 5.4.9. An integral with an irreducible quadratic.

    Consider
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\frac{x+2}{x^2+1}\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Solution.
    Split the integral:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\frac{x+2}{x^2+1}\,dx = \int\frac{x}{x^2+1}\,dx + 2\int\frac{1}{x^2+1}\,dx. \end{equation*}
    The first integral can be evaluated by substitution with \(u=x^2+1\text{.}\) The second involves the standard antiderivative associated with the inverse tangent function.
    🔗
